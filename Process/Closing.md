Moral Parsimony started as a question: can you make someone feel what it costs to simplify a moral framework? Months later, and 2 prototypes (practically one finished) I have a playable answer.

The game builds a procedural maze and gives the player a set of moral principles. Each principle does two things simultaneously: it reveals hidden pathways through the maze, and it grants a unique gameplay ability. At periodic checkpoints placed at intersection junctions, the player is forced to drop a principle. The maze responds immediately. Walls flood back in the dropped principle's color. The ability vanishes from the HUD. Paths the player was relying on close.

Simplification is never free. The maze is always solvable through its base corridors. The player will always reach the goal. But the experience of navigation becomes progressively thinner. Fewer shortcuts. Fewer tools. Fewer ways to see. Ultimately, engaging with this game mocks simplification of moral judgment.

This maps directly to moral parsimony as a philosophical concept. A simpler moral theory is more actionable, easier to apply, harder to get tangled in contradictions. But every principle you drop was doing work. It was revealing something about the situation that other principles could not. the closest and most "logical" avenue of thought may be efficient but is it the best option? 

Please do not think for a second I know any better, this game ATTEMPTED to scratch the surface of these questions, and I think it oversimplified the answer but maybe when I gain more experience in game design I can approach this with the respect it deserves.

### Future Refinements

The game is a functional prototype, not a finished product. Here is what I could pursue next:

**Sound design.** Each principle could carry an ambient audio signature, a tonal texture that fades when the principle is dropped. The maze should get quieter as the player simplifies. Sound would carry the thesis in a register the visuals cannot reach.

**Visual polish.** The isometric cube rendering is functional but flat. A shader pass for fog-of-war tied to distance from the player would create spatial atmosphere. Particle effects at thresholds and the goal would add ceremony to key moments. The floor tiles could subtly reflect principle colors in nearby corridors.

**A social cohesion mechanic.** The game could explore this through a multiplayer or NPC-adjacency layer: principles affect not just what paths you see, but how you encounter others in the maze. Dropping a principle could reduce NPC density or change how NPCs behave toward you.

**Procedural narrative.** The game world is set in a specific story or serious of events that are tied with a form of dramatic drive...where the maze is a cerebral view of the protagonist's attempts at making the best decisions under pressure to reach the "good ending". Maybe then the time-attack mechanic from the Unreal prototype can be meaningful.

The game works (bare-minimum).

Either way, the maze was always solvable....somehow.
